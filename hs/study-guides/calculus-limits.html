A tiny example with EVERY decision explained

NEW TRAINING EXAMPLE (NOT AN ORIGINAL EXAM QUESTION)
Find the limit as x approaches 3 of (x²−9)/(x−3).
1. Find what is asked: The limit (the approaching output) as x→3.
2. Classify: A fraction made of polynomials. Look at its denominator.
3. Try substituting x=3: Top=3²−9=0, bottom=3−3=0.
4. Interpret 0/0: It means "not settled," not "answer zero" and not "limit DNE".
5. Choose factoring: x²−9=(x−3)(x+3). We know this because (A²−B²)=(A−B)(A+B).
6. Rewrite the fraction: [(x−3)(x+3)]/(x−3).
7. Cancel: For values NEAR 3, x−3 is nonzero, so the matching factor cancels.
8. Simplify: The nearby expression is x+3.
9. Substitute again: 3+3=6.
LIMIT = 6. The original fraction has a hole at x=3, but its nearby heights approach 6.
Why this method rather than a "formula"? Substitution gave 0/0 and the expression contained an easy factor that canceled. That's the clue to factor first.
Both sides of the graph head toward the empty circle at y=6. The hole does not stop the limit from existing.

Algebra rescue kit

These questions look like calculus, but most mistakes actually happen in algebra. Learn these tiny moves first.

A. Divide by zero: what can and cannot happen

  1. 5/2 = 2.5: ordinary division works.
  2. 0/5 = 0: zero divided by a nonzero number works.
  3. 5/0: undefined. Near that point, a function may shoot up or down.
  4. 0/0: indeterminate. You cannot decide the limit yet; simplify the expression first.

B. Factoring: reversing multiplication

(x − 5)(x + 5) = x² − 25
  1. Recognize x² − 25 as a difference of squares.
  2. Write x² − 25 = (x − 5)(x + 5).
  3. If the same factor multiplies both top and bottom, cancel that factor for x-values where it is nonzero.
  4. The canceled point may remain a hole. Do not pretend the original denominator was defined there.

C. A safe cancellation example

(x − 5)/[(x − 5)(x + 5)] = 1/(x + 5), for x ≠ 5
Why canceling is legal near x = 5
A limit studies nearby x-values, not the exact target. All nearby x ≠ 5 can cancel (x − 5), even though x = 5 was originally forbidden.

D. Rationalizing a square root

(√x − √77)(√x + √77) = x − 77
  1. The conjugate keeps the same two terms but switches the minus sign to a plus.
  2. Multiply numerator and denominator by that same conjugate. This is multiplying by 1.
  3. The square-root difference becomes a regular difference, often letting you cancel.

E. Positive versus negative tiny numbers

1 / 0.001 = +1,000    |    1 / (−0.001) = −1,000
Memorize
A positive number divided by a tiny positive number grows toward +∞. A positive number divided by a tiny negative number grows toward −∞. Signs are essential.

What a limit really means

A limit is where the output is heading as the input gets close to a target. Think about walking toward a doorway without needing to stand exactly in it.

Limit notation decoded

lim[x → 3] f(x) = 5
  1. “x → 3” means the input gets closer to 3.
  2. “f(x)” means the rule’s output.
  3. “= 5” means the output approaches the height 5.
  4. The actual dot at x = 3 can be missing or at another height; that does not automatically change the limit.
A hole and a vertical asymptote are different. Near x = 10, the curve approaches a finite height even though that point is missing.

The first move: try plugging in the target

Plug in and get a number
Usually finish. This works for polynomials and other formulas continuous at the target.
Plug in and get 0/0
Do NOT say “does not exist.” Factor, cancel, or rationalize.
Nonzero / 0
Investigate an infinite limit. Check left/right signs.
x → ∞
Do not substitute infinity as a number. Use end behavior and highest powers.

Why left and right matter

lim[x → a⁻] f(x) = L   AND   lim[x → a⁺] f(x) = L

A two-sided limit exists and equals L only when both sides approach the same height L. If they head to different heights, the two-sided limit does not exist.

The three most common algebra limit types

These missed questions include a plain substitution, a factor-and-cancel limit, and a square-root/conjugate limit.

Type 1: nothing breaks when you substitute

lim[x → 2] (x² + 1) = 2² + 1 = 5
  1. Clue: polynomial (only powers added or subtracted).
  2. Substitute x = 2.
  3. Calculate 2² + 1 = 5.
  4. Answer: 5. Why: polynomials are continuous everywhere.

Type 2: 0/0 and matching factors

(x² − 25)/(x − 5) = [(x − 5)(x + 5)]/(x − 5)
  1. Clue: both top and bottom become 0 at x = 5.
  2. Factor the top using difference of squares.
  3. Cancel (x − 5) for nearby x-values that are not 5.
  4. The remaining expression is x + 5.
  5. Now the limit is 5 + 5 = 10.
  6. The original function still has a hole at x = 5.
The square-root exam question is another 0/0 type, but uses a conjugate instead of ordinary factoring.

Type 3: square root in the denominator

  1. Clue: a difference such as √x − √77 appears.
  2. Substitution at x = 77 gives 0/0.
  3. Multiply top and bottom by √x + √77.
  4. Use (a − b)(a + b) = a² − b².
  5. Cancel x − 77 before substituting.
  6. Then substitute x = 77.
Choosing between the two:
Polynomial factors? Try factoring.
Square-root difference? Try the conjugate.

One-sided limits, signs, and vertical walls

A vertical asymptote is a line where the graph shoots upward or downward without stopping. The left and right directions can be different.

Number-line signs: where are you approaching from?

x → 2⁻ : x is a little smaller than 2, so x − 2 < 0
x → 2⁺ : x is a little larger than 2, so x − 2 > 0
The graph plunges downward from the RIGHT of x = 2, because its numerator is negative and denominator tiny positive.
  1. If you see a nonzero number divided by 0, find the sign of the numerator near the target.
  2. Determine the sign of the denominator to the LEFT.
  3. Determine the sign of the denominator to the RIGHT.
  4. Use (+)/(+) = + and (−)/(+) = −, etc.
  5. When the denominator gets tiny, the absolute size becomes enormous.
  6. If signs disagree between sides, the two-sided limit does not exist.

Hole versus vertical asymptote

At x=10, a factor cancels and produces a hole. At x=−10, the remaining denominator is zero and the graph has a vertical asymptote.
Exam trap: A denominator of zero does not by itself guarantee a vertical asymptote. Cancel shared factors FIRST, then see what is still in the denominator.

An even power stays positive

x⁴ > 0 for every x ≠ 0; for x close to 0, x⁴ is tiny positive

This is why 1/[x⁴(x+5)] approaches +∞ from both sides of zero. Even powers remove the minus sign of a negative input.

Infinity means “look very far away”

When x grows extremely large, the biggest power of x usually controls a polynomial fraction. Think of comparing a giant number to a much smaller one.

Powers: which part grows fastest?

x⁶ grows faster than x⁵ as x → +∞
Biggest degreeWhat to expect for rational fractions
Top degree smallerLimit = 0; horizontal asymptote y = 0.
Top degree equalLimit = leading coefficient on top / leading coefficient below.
Top degree largerUsually grows without bound; check positive and negative direction for the sign.
Example: x⁻¹⁶ is another name for 1/x¹⁶; it approaches zero as x gets large.

A minus exponent means “flip”

x⁻¹⁶ = 1/x¹⁶    (x ≠ 0)
  1. Do not misread −16 as “negative x.”
  2. Rewrite a negative exponent as 1 over a positive exponent.
  3. As x gets large, x¹⁶ gets huge.
  4. One divided by a huge positive number gets close to 0.

Logarithms: slow but unbounded

ln(1) = 0;   ln(e) = 1;   ln(x) only works for x > 0
The logarithm curve for f(x)=4−ln x grows to +∞ near x=0 from the right, but decreases without bound for x→∞.
  1. Near x = 0 from the right, ln(x) goes to −∞.
  2. Subtracting a very negative quantity makes 4 − ln(x) go to +∞.
  3. As x → ∞, ln(x) goes to +∞.
  4. So 4 − ln(x) goes to −∞.

Continuity: can you draw it without lifting your pencil?

A continuous graph has no breaks at the point you are checking. “No break” has three precise mathematical requirements.

The three-item continuity checklist

#RequirementPlain-English question
1f(a) is definedIs there a filled dot / allowed formula value at a?
2lim[x→a] f(x) existsDo the LEFT and RIGHT sides approach the same height?
3lim[x→a] f(x) = f(a)Is that approaching height the height of the filled dot?

Three types of break

Open circles mean a missing point; filled circles mean actual function values. A jump is when the sides approach different heights.
NameWhat you seeRepairable by setting one dot?
Removable holeBoth sides approach the same height; point is absent or at wrong height.Yes.
JumpLeft and right approach different finite heights.No.
Vertical asymptoteValues increase/decrease without bound near a vertical line.No.

Piecewise functions: the rule changes

  1. Clue: a giant curly brace with rules such as “if x<0” and “if x≥0.”
  2. Choose the rule for LEFT-side input values.
  3. Choose the rule for RIGHT-side input values.
  4. Evaluate each side separately.
  5. Check if both sides agree.
  6. To test continuity, also check which branch defines f(a).
At x=0 the left side heads to 5 but the right side heads to 0. The filled dot at 0 is on the right-hand branch.

A special theorem: polynomials are continuous everywhere

If the function has only added or multiplied powers of x, such as 5x⁶+7x²+4, you do not need to inspect every possible x separately: it is continuous for all real numbers.

A fraction may be undefined where its denominator is zero. A square root of a real number requires its inside to be nonnegative. ln(x) requires x>0. Check these restrictions before saying “continuous everywhere.”

Trig functions, the IVT, and slope

These are three short special skill groups. They feel different, but each has a very recognizable cue.

A. sec(x): a reciprocal trig function

sec(x) = 1/cos(x)
  1. “cos” is the cosine function.
  2. “sec” is one divided by cosine.
  3. When cos(x)=0, sec(x) is undefined.
  4. Memorize: cos(π/3)=1/2, so sec(π/3)=2.
  5. Cosine is zero at π/2 + kπ for any integer k; sec is continuous between those excluded points.
The red dashed vertical lines mark where cosine equals zero, making sec undefined.

B. Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT): the no-teleporting rule

Imagine a person walking smoothly from below the ground floor to above it. If they never teleport, they must pass ground level somewhere. That is the big idea behind the IVT.

The continuous curve starts below 0 at x=1 and ends above 0 at x=2. It must cross y=0 between those points.
  1. Check that the function is continuous on the full closed interval.
  2. Calculate the function at the left endpoint.
  3. Calculate the function at the right endpoint.
  4. See whether your target number lies between the two results.
  5. If the target is 0 and one endpoint is negative while the other is positive, a solution is guaranteed in between.
  6. The IVT proves a solution exists, but does not automatically tell you its exact location.

C. Secant line = average rate of change

slope m = [s(t₂) − s(t₁)] / (t₂ − t₁)
SymbolMeans
t₁, t₂Starting and ending times.
s(t₁), s(t₂)Positions at those times.
mSlope of the secant: average change in position per unit time.
πPi ≈ 3.14159; use RADIANS for sin(πt).
The orange secant joins two points on the blue motion curve. Its slope is the object’s average velocity over that time interval.
  1. Read the two time values.
  2. Calculate the position at each time.
  3. Subtract end position minus start position.
  4. Subtract end time minus start time.
  5. Divide position change by time change.
  6. A positive slope means average motion in the positive direction; negative means average motion in the negative direction.

Recognize 0/0 and factor

NEW WORKED EXAMPLE
lim as x→4 of (x² − 16)/(x − 4)
Because x−4 cancels for x near 4, the graph follows y=x+4 with a hole at (4, 8). Both sides still approach 8.
1. ASK: A finite limit. The input x heads toward 4.
2. CLASSIFY: A polynomial fraction, so try substituting 4 first.
3. TEST: Top 16 − 16 = 0; bottom 4 − 4 = 0. This gives 0/0, NOT a final answer.
4. CHOOSE: The numerator is a difference of squares; factoring can expose a matching factor.
5. WORK: x² − 16 = (x − 4)(x + 4).
6. WORK: [(x − 4)(x + 4)]/(x − 4) = x + 4 for x ≠ 4.
7. FINISH: Now x→4 gives 4 + 4 = 8.
8. CHECK: The graph would have a hole at (4,8), but nearby heights approach 8.

Recognize piecewise boundaries

NEW WORKED EXAMPLE
f(x)=2x+3 for x<1; f(x)=x²+1 for x≥1. Is f continuous at 1?
Compare a hole, a jump, and an infinite discontinuity. Piecewise left/right mismatch is a jump.
1. ASK: Continuity at boundary x=1; left and right must be checked.
2. CLASSIFY: Piecewise: two formulas apply on different sides.
3. WORK: From LEFT use 2x+3, giving 2(1)+3 = 5.
4. WORK: From RIGHT use x²+1, giving 1+1=2.
5. COMPARE: Left 5 ≠ right 2, so two-sided limit DNE.
6. FINISH: Continuity fails even though f(1)=2 is defined.

Recognize an average-rate question

NEW WORKED EXAMPLE
Position s(t)=t²+2t meters. Find average velocity from t=1 s to t=3 s.
Secant slope = change in height divided by change in horizontal distance, here position per time.
1. ASK: Average velocity means change in position divided by elapsed time.
2. CHOOSE: Use slope: [s(b) − s(a)]/(b − a), where a=1 and b=3.
3. WORK: s(1)=1² + 2(1)=3 meters.
4. WORK: s(3)=3² + 2(3)=15 meters.
5. WORK: Change in position = 15 − 3 = 12 meters.
6. WORK: Time elapsed = 3 − 1 = 2 seconds.
7. FINISH: Average velocity = 12 m / 2 s = 6 m/s.
8. CHECK: Velocity is in meters per second, not meters.

1. Finite limit with a fixed parameter

ORIGINAL QUESTION (RESTATED)
Find the limit or state DNE. b is a fixed real number:
lim[x→b] {[(x−b)¹⁰ − x + b] / (x−b)}

FIRST, learn how to choose the method (do this before calculating)

1WHAT AM I BEING ASKED? The arrow says x goes toward b. The letter b is FIXED, but no number for b is needed.
2WHAT TYPE? Rational expression with a factor that becomes zero.
3FIRST TEST / GIVEN CLUE: First test x=b: top 0 and bottom 0.
4WHICH METHOD AND WHY? 0/0 means factor: rewrite −x+b as −(x−b), then cancel the shared x−b.
5WHAT SHOULD THE ANSWER LOOK LIKE? After canceling, plug x=b into the simpler nearby rule.
COMMON WRONG TURN TO AVOID: Do not divide by 0 or choose a made-up number for b.
LOOK FOR THIS CLUE
A letter b is fixed, and x approaches b; the denominator x−b would become zero.
WHY THIS METHOD
Rename x−b as h. Then x→b means h→0. This avoids dividing by zero.
Symbol / valueMeaning in THIS question
bFixed real number named in question; can be any real value.
xInput approaching b.
hTemporary helper h=x−b; approaches 0.

Every step, explained line by line

  1. Begin with [(x−b)¹⁰ − x + b] / (x−b).
  2. Notice −x + b = −(x−b).
  3. Rewrite the top: (x−b)¹⁰ − (x−b).
  4. Factor (x−b): (x−b)[(x−b)⁹ − 1].
  5. Cancel (x−b), valid for nearby x≠b.
  6. Now the expression is (x−b)⁹ − 1.
  1. As x→b, (x−b)→0.
  2. Calculate 0⁹ − 1 = −1.
FINAL ANSWER: −1
Does it make sense? The limit is the same for every fixed real b. The original formula is undefined at x=b, but that does not prevent a finite limit.

2. Hole or asymptote

ORIGINAL QUESTION (RESTATED)
For f(x)=(x−10)/(x²−100), find:
(a) lim[x→10] f(x)
(b) lim[x→−10⁻] f(x)
(c) lim[x→−10⁺] f(x)
Find all vertical asymptotes.

FIRST, learn how to choose the method (do this before calculating)

1WHAT AM I BEING ASKED? There are three limits: approach 10; approach −10 from left; approach −10 from right.
2WHAT TYPE? Rational fraction: possible hole and vertical asymptote.
3FIRST TEST / GIVEN CLUE: x²−100 factors into (x−10)(x+10).
4WHICH METHOD AND WHY? Cancel shared (x−10) for the limit near 10; near −10 inspect the remaining 1/(x+10) sign.
5WHAT SHOULD THE ANSWER LOOK LIKE? At 10 expect a finite limit; at −10 the two sides may approach opposite infinities.
COMMON WRONG TURN TO AVOID: A canceled zero gives a hole, not a vertical asymptote.
What the mathematical answer looks like visually.
LOOK FOR THIS CLUE
The denominator factors; investigate two target values and different sides.
WHY THIS METHOD
Factor difference of squares first. Look for a canceling factor (hole) versus a remaining zero (vertical asymptote).
Symbol / valueMeaning in THIS question
xInput number.
f(x)Output of the rational function.

Every step, explained line by line

  1. Factor the denominator: x²−100 = (x−10)(x+10).
  2. Write f(x)=(x−10)/[(x−10)(x+10)].
  3. For x≠10 and x≠−10, cancel (x−10).
  4. Simplified nearby rule: f(x)=1/(x+10).
  5. (a) x→10: 1/(10+10)=1/20=0.05.
  6. (b) x→−10 from LEFT: x+10 is tiny NEGATIVE.
  7. Positive 1 divided by tiny negative → −∞.
  8. (c) x→−10 from RIGHT: x+10 is tiny POSITIVE.
  1. Positive 1 divided by tiny positive → +∞.
  2. At x=10 the original rule has a hole; at x=−10 the function has a vertical asymptote.
FINAL ANSWER: (a) 1/20; (b) −∞; (c) +∞. Vertical asymptote: x=−10 only.
Does it make sense? A hole at x=10 is NOT a vertical asymptote; the factor causing it canceled.

3. One-sided infinite limit

ORIGINAL QUESTION (RESTATED)
Find lim[x→2⁺] (x²−4x+3)/(x−2).

FIRST, learn how to choose the method (do this before calculating)

1WHAT AM I BEING ASKED? x→2⁺ means approach 2 using numbers just GREATER than 2.
2WHAT TYPE? One-sided limit of a fraction.
3FIRST TEST / GIVEN CLUE: At x=2 the numerator is −1 but denominator is 0.
4WHICH METHOD AND WHY? Check whether x−2 is tiny positive or tiny negative; right side makes it positive.
5WHAT SHOULD THE ANSWER LOOK LIKE? Use sign arithmetic: negative ÷ tiny positive becomes negative without bound.
COMMON WRONG TURN TO AVOID: Do not factor just because there is a denominator; inspect the numerator first.
What the mathematical answer looks like visually.
LOOK FOR THIS CLUE
x→2⁺ asks about RIGHT only; substituting gives nonzero/0.
WHY THIS METHOD
Use signs of numerator and denominator near 2. Do not factor to invent cancellation.
Symbol / valueMeaning in THIS question
x→2⁺Inputs just bigger than 2.
∞Unbounded positive growth.

Every step, explained line by line

  1. Numerator at x=2: 2² − 4(2) + 3 = 4 − 8 + 3 = −1.
  2. Denominator x−2 near 2 from RIGHT is a tiny POSITIVE number.
  3. The quotient looks like (−1)/(tiny positive).
  1. Negative divided by tiny positive becomes huge negative.
  2. Therefore the one-sided limit is −∞.
FINAL ANSWER: −∞
Does it make sense? Try x=2.01: numerator≈−1, denominator 0.01, so output≈−100 (large negative).

ORIGINAL QUESTION (RESTATED)
Determine the intervals where f(x)=sec(x) is continuous. Find lim[x→π/3] sec(x) and lim[x→(3π/2)⁻] sec(x).

FIRST, learn how to choose the method (do this before calculating)

1WHAT AM I BEING ASKED? The word "continuous" asks where sec x has no breaks; two limits test special angles.
2WHAT TYPE? Trigonometric reciprocal function with vertical asymptotes.
3FIRST TEST / GIVEN CLUE: sec x means 1/cos x.
4WHICH METHOD AND WHY? Use cosine values and check signs near where cosine is zero.
5WHAT SHOULD THE ANSWER LOOK LIKE? The function works whenever cos x is nonzero.

What the mathematical answer looks like visually.
LOOK FOR THIS CLUE
sec(x) means reciprocal cosine; find undefined points and limits at a regular point and a vertical wall.
WHY THIS METHOD
Rewrite sec(x)=1/cos(x). Cosine is zero at π/2+kπ; determine the sign near 3π/2 from the left.
Symbol / valueMeaning in THIS question
sec(x)1/cos(x).
πPi≈3.14159; angle measured in radians.
kAny integer: …,−2,−1,0,1,2,…

Every step, explained line by line
