BEFORE THE FIRST EXAMPLE — SYMBOLS, UNITS & VALUES TO MEMORIZE

Read this before any formula or physics example

A variable is a symbol representing a quantity. A symbol's value may change from one question to another. The unit tells what is being measured, and a standard value is a number you may need to remember.

MEMORIZE: the symbol, meaning, unit, and validity conditions for standard near-Earth values and exact unit definitions.

GIVEN: a number in the question, such as a building height of 85 m, is not a universal constant.

SOLVE: a symbol marked with ? is the quantity you must calculate.

CONDITION: an equation only works under its stated assumptions, such as constant acceleration or negligible air resistance.

Memorize the meaning and units of the variables together with the equation. Memorizing an equation without its assumptions can make you select the wrong one. You can derive or rearrange many formulas instead of learning every algebraic version as a separate fact.

What NOT to memorize as isolated facts

Do not memorize a fixed answer like “ramp acceleration is 5.6” or “a 5-kg and 3-kg pulley accelerates at 2.45.” Those numbers change when the givens change. Memorize the relationships, then rebuild the solution from the givens.

START HERE: Physics from absolutely zero

Start with the symbol-and-constant reference on the immediately preceding pages. This beginner lesson uses F, m, a, s, g, t, and θ, which have already been defined there.

Physics is the study of how things move, interact, transfer energy, and behave. You do not have to know its abbreviations in advance. The course defines them before using them. Each solution puts one mathematical action on a new line.

1. An equation is a sentence, and each letter has a meaning

A symbol such as F or m is a placeholder for a measured quantity. The letter alone never tells you the units; the definition does.

  1. Identify the object: the cart, with mass m = 4 kilograms (kg). Mass indicates how resistant a body is to changes in velocity.
  2. Identify the net force: F = 12 newtons (N) acting horizontally. “Net” means all forces combined along that direction.
  3. Identify the unknown: a = acceleration, measured in meters per second squared (m/s²).
  4. Choose F = ma because the question gives net force and mass and asks for acceleration.
  5. Divide both sides by m: F/m = (ma)/m.
  6. Cancel m/m = 1: a = F/m.
  7. Substitute: a = 12 N / 4 kg.
  8. Convert the unit: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²; cancel kg to leave m/s².
  9. Calculate: 12/4 = 3, so a = 3 m/s².

2. Why the unit m/s² is not the same as m/s

Speed tells you how fast something moves. Acceleration tells you how quickly its velocity changes. The second “per second” in m/s² makes the distinction.

  1. Read 3 m/s² aloud as “three meters per second, each second.”
  2. At time t = 0 seconds, the cart has velocity 0 m/s if it starts from rest.
  3. After 1 second, the cart’s velocity is 3 m/s.
  4. After 2 seconds, the cart’s velocity is 6 m/s.
  5. After 3 seconds, the cart’s velocity is 9 m/s.
  6. Do not confuse the velocity values with the distance traveled: a speed of 9 m/s is not a distance of 9 m.

3. Learn algebra with a balanced equation

A formula is an equality: left and right have the same value. If you divide the left by m, you must divide the right by m. Algebra is how you isolate what you need.

  1. Write the original equation without plugging in values: F = m × a.
  2. Circle the unknown letter: a.
  3. Notice that m is multiplied by a.
  4. Use the inverse of multiplication, which is division.
  5. Divide both sides by m: F/m = (m × a)/m.
  6. Cancel the m values on the right: m/m = 1.
  7. Rewrite in the common form: a = F/m.
  8. Replace symbols with units and numbers only after the algebra is correct.
  9. Check by multiplying your result a by m; it should reproduce the given net force F.

4. Square roots undo squaring (free fall example)

Falling distance grows faster and faster because gravity changes velocity each second. When an object starts with no vertical velocity, its vertical distance traveled is s = ½gt².

  1. Write s = ½gt²; define s = 85 meters and g = 9.8 m/s².
  2. Identify the unknown t, elapsed time in seconds.
  3. Multiply BOTH sides by 2: 2s = gt².
  4. Divide BOTH sides by g: 2s/g = t².
  5. Take the square root of BOTH sides: √(2s/g) = |t|.
  6. Because elapsed time is nonnegative, choose t = √(2s/g).
  7. Substitute: t = √[(2 × 85 m)/(9.8 m/s²)].
  8. Divide inside the root: t = √(17.3469 s²).
  9. Take the square root: t ≈ 4.16 s.
  10. Check by squaring 4.16 and multiplying by 4.9: approximately 85 meters.

5. What inverse trigonometry does

Sine, cosine, and tangent connect side lengths and angles in a right triangle. The inverse versions sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹, and tan⁻¹ answer “which angle creates this ratio?”

  1. Label the right angle (90°).
  2. Choose the angle θ you need to find.
  3. Identify the opposite side as the side across from θ.
  4. Identify the adjacent side as the leg touching θ.
  5. Identify the hypotenuse as the longest side, across from 90°.
  6. If opposite = 3 m and adjacent = 4 m, choose tan θ = opposite/adjacent.
  7. Substitute: tan θ = 3/4 = 0.75.
  8. Undo the tangent operation with inverse tangent: θ = tan⁻¹(0.75).
  9. Set the calculator to DEGREES mode.
  10. Calculate: θ ≈ 36.87°.
  11. Check: tan(36.87°) ≈ 0.75. Note that tan⁻¹ is NOT 1/tan.

6. Memorize the nine-line habit, one step per line

This checklist is the basic skill required for the test. A new problem might use different numbers or wording, but the process remains the same.

  1. READ: underline numbers and units, then circle what is requested.
  2. DRAW: sketch the object, movement, forces, triangle, or circuit.
  3. CLASSIFY: decide whether this is precision, units, vectors, motion, forces, or circuits.
  4. SELECT: find the equation that contains what you know and what you need.
  5. JUSTIFY: write the physical assumptions that make your equation valid.
  6. REARRANGE: isolate the unknown with algebra before substituting.
  7. SUBSTITUTE: replace each symbol with the known value AND its unit.
  8. COMPUTE: keep extra digits and round only at the end.
  9. CHECK: inspect unit cancellation, sign, direction, magnitude, and physical meaning.

THE TEST-QUESTION DECISION SYSTEM

This is the central strategy of the whole course. Do not begin by searching your memory for a random formula. First decide what the question asks, then classify the situation, identify the clues and restrictions, and finally select the equation.

COMPREHENSION GOAL: Without looking at the answers, explain (1) the question type, (2) what every variable means, (3) why the equation applies, (4) the calculation with units, and (5) what the result tells you physically.

Nine lines to write on scratch paper for every problem

  1. WHAT IS ASKED? Write the unknown, such as a = ? m/s².
  2. CLASSIFY: Name the physics topic AND the subtype, such as “forces: inclined plane with friction.”
  3. GIVENS: Copy each number and its unit; define its symbol.
  4. CONDITIONS: Record frictionless, at rest, angle, constant velocity, height, circuit wiring, etc.
  5. DRAW: Sketch the physical situation and label the given values.
  6. CHOOSE: Pick an equation containing the unknown and enough known quantities.
  7. JUSTIFY + REARRANGE: State the assumptions; isolate the unknown before entering numbers.
  8. SUBSTITUTE + COMPUTE: Keep the units visible, calculate carefully, and round only at the end.
  9. CHECK: Confirm units, size, sign, direction, and physical plausibility.

ADDED LESSON  Understand the concepts before attempting the worksheet

Concept visuals — picture the physics before the equation

A measurement is not an exact number

35.864 might come from a precise measurement, while 7.2 has only one decimal place. Even if a calculator prints 28.664, the measured data do not support all three decimal digits.

  1. Identify the mathematical operation.
  2. For addition or subtraction, find the input with the fewest decimal places.
  3. For multiplication or division, find the input with the fewest significant digits.
  4. Keep full calculator precision until the very end.
  5. Round the result at the correct place.
  6. For ambiguous trailing zeros, use scientific notation to make the number of significant digits clear.

How to count digits

Nonzero digits count. Zeros between nonzero digits count. Leading zeros do not count. Trailing zeros after a decimal point count.

  1. Example: 0.00450 contains three significant digits: 4, 5, and the final 0.
  2. Example: 4.0 contains two significant digits.
  3. Example: 4000 without a decimal point can be ambiguous; 4.0 × 10³ clearly contains two significant digits.
  4. When adding, align the decimal points and choose the least precise place.
  5. When multiplying, count the significant digits in each measured factor.

Unit cancellation is algebra

You can multiply by a fraction representing the number 1 to swap the units without changing the physical quantity.

  1. Write the starting number with its original unit.
  2. Write a conversion ratio that contains both the old and new unit.
  3. Put the unwanted unit where it cancels (top against bottom).
  4. Multiply and divide only after cancellation is visible.
  5. Confirm that the remaining unit matches the question.

Length, area, and volume are different

A room floor is area (length × width), whereas a box or fish tank has volume (length × width × height). That is why area scales with the square and volume with the cube.

  1. For length, convert by a factor of 100 from m to cm.
  2. For area, convert by 100 × 100 = 10,000.
  3. For volume, convert by 100 × 100 × 100 = 1,000,000.
  4. For acceleration from seconds squared to hours squared, square the time conversion too.
  5. Never guess whether a factor should be squared or cubed: look at the exponent on the unit.

Concept visuals — picture the physics before the equation

Horizontal and vertical motions share the same clock

Choosing x and y as separate directions makes a complicated curved path into two simpler equations.

  1. Draw the launch point and the landing point.
  2. Choose positive x forward and positive y up.
  3. If thrown horizontally, set initial vertical velocity v₀y = 0.
  4. If launched at angle θ, resolve v₀x = v₀cosθ and v₀y = v₀sinθ.
  5. Write vertical displacement Δy = v₀y t − ½gt².
  6. Solve the vertical equation for t first when finding landing position.
  7. Compute horizontal distance Δx = v₀x t.
  8. For final velocity, compute vᵧ = v₀y − gt then combine x and y components.

Maximum height and the cliff exception

At the top of a trajectory, vertical velocity is zero but gravity is still acting. A cliff landing is below the launch point, so flight time is NOT simply twice the time to the top.

  1. To find maximum height, set vᵧ = 0 in vᵧ² = v₀y² − 2gΔy.
  2. Compute the rise ABOVE the launch point: Δy = v₀y²/(2g).
  3. If the problem asks height ABOVE THE GROUND, add the launch height.
  4. For landing at a different height, solve the full vertical-position equation.
  5. Only use total time = 2v₀y/g when landing and launch heights are equal.

Special case: The original angled-launch problem does not explicitly say landing height. We state the same-height assumption when using a symmetric total flight time.

Concept visuals — picture the physics before the equation

How to make a free-body diagram

An FBD isolates a single object. Draw arrows only for forces actually acting ON that object. The arrow direction matters more than artistic accuracy.

  1. Draw one box or dot to represent the chosen object.
  2. Draw weight mg straight down.
  3. Draw normal force N perpendicular out from the surface if there is contact.
  4. Draw tension T along any rope, pulling away from the object.
  5. Draw friction fₖ opposite the direction of sliding.
  6. Split angled applied forces into x and y using cosine and sine.
  7. Write ΣFx = maₓ and ΣFy = maᵧ with a consistent positive direction.

Incline, pull, push, pulley, and elevator

Choose coordinate axes that match the geometry. On a ramp the helpful axes are along and perpendicular to the slope. On level ground, the pull or push may change the normal force.

  1. Incline: use downhill mg sinθ and into-slope mg cosθ.
  2. Incline: find N = mg cosθ if there is no perpendicular acceleration.
  3. Incline: find kinetic friction fₖ = μₖN; subtract it from downhill motion.
  4. Pulling upward: find N = mg − F sinθ on a level floor.
  5. Pushing downward: find N = mg + F sinθ on a level floor.
  6. Pulley: draw a separate FBD for each mass and one acceleration magnitude for both.
  7. Elevator scale: read normal force N rather than mg; use N − mg = ma.

Concept visuals — picture the physics before the equation

Ohm’s law and power

For ideal resistors at a fixed temperature, V=IR. For electrical power, P=IV. Choose the equation that contains the requested quantity and the given ones.

  1. Underline given voltages V, currents I, resistances R, and power P.
  2. If V and R are given and I is requested, divide to get I=V/R.
  3. If V and I are given and R is requested, divide to get R=V/I.
  4. If P and V are given and I is requested, divide P by V.
  5. Check that volts/ohms equals amps, and watts/volts equals amps.

Series, parallel, and combination rules

Circuits are determined by connectivity, NOT how the picture is drawn on the page. Identify whether an element lies on one route or in a branch.

  1. Trace from one battery terminal to the other.
  2. If every component is along one path, the components are in series.
  3. In series, current is the same in each; equivalent R adds.
  4. If multiple branches join the same two nodes, the branches are parallel.
  5. In parallel, voltage is the same in each; branch currents add.
  6. For a combination circuit, simplify one series or parallel group at a time.
  7. Then work backward to find each branch voltage or current.

Common traps and how to avoid them

  • Significant figures: do not apply decimal-place rounding to multiplication or significant-digit rounding to subtraction.
  • Units: square or cube both the numeric conversion and the unit; for accelerations, convert time squared.
  • Vectors: add x to x and y to y, then find magnitude; the sum of lengths is generally wrong.
  • Angles: keep calculator in DEG mode; use inverse trig for an unknown angle; confirm quadrant.
  • Projectiles: horizontal and vertical share time, not velocity; at the apex vertical velocity is zero but gravity is not.
  • Cliffs: do not double time-to-peak unless landing is at the same elevation as launch.
  • Free-body diagrams: normal is perpendicular to surface; friction opposes motion; gravity is always vertically down.
  • Atwood machines: connected objects share acceleration magnitude; one tension for an ideal rope; draw an FBD for each mass.
  • Circuits: in series, current same; in parallel, voltage same. A parallel equivalent R must be less than its smallest branch R.
